**Title:** Are your bundles raising AOV, or just discounting orders that were already coming in?

Something I keep seeing in store audits: a brand launches a bundle at a flat percentage off, AOV goes up, and everyone calls it a win. Then you look at who actually bought the bundle. Look at your order data for the product people most often buy next, usually within a few weeks of the first order. Then price that add-on so it feels almost free when it's attached to the main item, instead of spreading one discount across the whole cart.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How do I create product bundles that actually increase average order value without hurting my margins?

The short answer: build the bundle around a second purchase your customer is already close to making, and put the discount on that add-on instead of across the whole cart.

Here is why that matters. The most common bundle setup is a flat discount, say 20% off a set of products. It usually does raise AOV. But when you look at who buys it, many of those customers would have bought the full set at full price anyway. You haven't created a bigger order. You've discounted one you had already won, and that shows up as lost margin rather than lost revenue, so it's easy to miss.

A better process:

1. Find the natural next purchase. Go through your order history and look for the product customers most often buy second, ideally within a few weeks of their first order. That is the purchase they are already leaning toward.

2. Anchor the bundle on your core product at full price. The item people come to you for doesn't need a discount.

3. Make the add-on feel almost free. Concentrate the incentive on the second item so the bundle reads as "I'm getting this extra thing for very little," not "everything is a bit cheaper."

4. Measure margin per order, not just AOV. Compare bundle buyers against customers who bought the same products separately at full price. If the groups look alike, the bundle is cannibalizing sales.

5. Watch repeat behavior. A good bundle pulls a future purchase forward. A bad one just trains customers to wait for the discount.

I walked through a real client example of this, a skincare routine bundle, in more detail here if it's useful: https://siavashsed.github.io/ecommerceedge/product-bundle-strategy-average-order-value/

The main point is that a bundle should change what a customer buys, not just what they pay.
