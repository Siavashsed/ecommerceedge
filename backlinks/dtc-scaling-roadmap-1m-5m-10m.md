**Title:** Why do most DTC brands fix the wrong thing at each revenue stage?

I've been consulting for DTC brands for a while, and the same pattern keeps showing up: founders apply the playbook for the wrong stage.

Here's the version I've seen play out. A skincare client spent $38,000 on Meta in a single March. Their ROAS fell from 2.9 to 1.6, and the founder was sure the creative had gone stale. The real problem was the checkout, which showed a shipping surprise on step three. More ad spend just sent more people into a leaky funnel.

My rough rule of thumb:

- Around $1M, fix conversion and margin. Checkout friction, pricing, shipping thresholds, unit economics.
- Around $5M, fix retention. Email and SMS flows, repeat purchase rate, subscription or replenishment offers.
- Around $10M, fix the team and the channel mix. Who owns what, and how much you depend on one paid channel.

Most brands do it backwards. They hire a big team or chase new channels at $1M, then discover at $5M that their repeat rate is weak. That order costs about a year.

Curious what others here have seen. Did you ever fix the wrong thing first, and what did it cost you?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** What should a DTC ecommerce brand focus on at $1M, $5M, and $10M in revenue?

The short answer is that each revenue stage has a different bottleneck, and most brands lose time by working on the wrong one.

Around $1M, focus on conversion and margin. You probably don't have a traffic problem yet. You have a leak problem. Look at your checkout, shipping costs, pricing, and contribution margin after ads. I once watched a skincare client spend $38,000 on Meta in a single March while their checkout showed a shipping surprise on step three. ROAS fell from 2.9 to 1.6, and the founder blamed the creative. The ads weren't the issue. The checkout was.

Around $5M, focus on retention. By this point, acquiring customers gets more expensive, so the money is in getting existing customers to buy again. Build solid email and SMS flows, measure repeat purchase rate and time between orders, and consider replenishment or subscription offers where they fit the product. A small lift in repeat rate usually beats a large increase in ad spend.

Around $10M, focus on the team and the channel mix. The founder can no longer be the bottleneck, so clear ownership across marketing, operations, and customer experience matters a great deal. You also want to reduce reliance on any single paid channel, whether that's Meta, Google, or an affiliate partner, because concentration risk gets expensive at this size.

The mistake I see most often is doing it backwards: hiring a big team or chasing new channels at $1M, then finding at $5M that nobody comes back for a second order. That reversal typically costs about a year.

Whichever stage you're at, start by finding the single biggest leak before adding more spend.
