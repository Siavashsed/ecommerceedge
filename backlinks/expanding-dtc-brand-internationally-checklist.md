**Title:** Launched a DTC brand into 3 countries in one week and 31 parcels got stuck in customs. What I'd do differently?

In March 2023 I launched a candle brand into Germany, the UK and Australia in the same week. It felt ambitious. It was a mistake.

We shipped duties-unpaid (DAP), so by day nine 31 parcels were sitting in customs. Buyers were being hit with roughly $22 in duties and fees on a $48 order. Many refused delivery or demanded refunds, and that month's margin was gone.

Three things I'd do differently:

1. Pick ONE market, not five. Choose the one where customers already live. Check your analytics and look at where you already get orders, traffic and email signups from outside your home country.

2. Ship duties-paid (DDP) so the price at checkout is the final price. Surprise fees at the door are what kill conversion and trigger chargebacks.

3. Treat the first market as a boring test. You're validating shipping times, returns, tax registration and support, not chasing a big launch.

The exciting markets are usually the wrong first move. The boring one, the one that's already half your international traffic, is where you learn cheaply.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How should a DTC brand decide which country to expand into first?

Short answer: pick one market, and make it the one where your customers already live. Not the one that looks biggest on a slide.

I'm Nina Patel, and I advise DTC brands on scaling. The most common mistake I see is treating international expansion as a launch event. I made it myself. In March 2023 I launched a candle brand into Germany, the UK and Australia in the same week. By day nine, 31 parcels were stuck in customs because we'd shipped duties-unpaid. Buyers were being billed about $22 on a $48 order, and the refunds wiped out the month's margin.

Here's the process I'd use now:

1. Mine your own data first. Look at which non-home countries already send you orders, traffic, email signups and repeat buyers. Existing demand beats projected demand.

2. Check the operational basics. Shipping time, carrier reliability, return costs, tax or VAT registration, and language needs. A market with strong demand but painful logistics is not a good first test.

3. Decide on duties before you launch. Duties-paid checkout, where the customer sees the final price, avoids the surprise fees that cause refusals and chargebacks.

4. Start with one market and treat it as a controlled experiment. Set clear targets for conversion, delivery time and return rate, and don't open another market until those hold.

5. Expand in sequence. Once the first market runs cleanly, the second reuses most of your playbook at much lower risk.

The boring choice usually wins. It teaches you the most for the least money.
