**Title:** Why do DTC brands keep stocking out of their best seller and overstocking the rest at the same time?

Something I keep seeing with DTC brands: stockouts and dead stock get treated as two separate problems. Usually they come from the same mistake, which is building the forecast from last year's sales totals.

Last year's sales are not last year's demand. Say your best seller was out of stock for three weeks. Those weeks show low sales, so the forecast learns to order less, and you stock out again. Meanwhile a slow SKU that got carried by a promo bundle looks healthier than it is, so you reorder too much of it.

What has worked better for the brands I've looked at:

1. Rebuild demand for the stockout weeks. Use the sell rate from the days it was in stock, not the zeros.
2. Use your actual supplier lead time from the last few POs, not the quoted one.
3. Put your own marketing calendar into the forecast. If you know a launch email or a creator push is coming, that demand is predictable.

None of this needs fancy software. A spreadsheet gets you most of the way.

Curious how others handle the promo calendar part. Do you forecast it separately or bake it into the baseline?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How should a DTC brand forecast inventory to avoid both stockouts and dead stock?

I work with DTC brands on scaling. The most common forecasting mistake I see is ordering from last year's sales totals. It feels safe because it's real data, but it measures the wrong thing.

Sales only tell you what you were able to sell, not what customers wanted. If your top SKU was out of stock for a month, that month records low sales, and a forecast built on it orders too little, so you stock out again. A slow product that got a lift from a bundle or a discount looks stronger than it is, so you order too much. That's why stockouts and dead stock so often show up together. They're the same error going in opposite directions.

Here is the approach I recommend:

1. Estimate true demand. For any period when a SKU was out of stock, replace the zeros with the sell rate from the days it was in stock. Strip out one-off spikes that won't come back.

2. Use real lead times. Take the average of your last several purchase orders, from PO date to stock available to sell, and add a buffer for variability. Quoted lead times are almost always optimistic.

3. Put your marketing calendar into the forecast. Launches, email campaigns, creator collaborations and sales events are demand you create yourself, so you can plan for it. Forecast those lifts on purpose instead of treating them as surprises.

4. Review often. A monthly check of forecast against actuals by SKU catches drift before it turns into a stockout or a warehouse full of slow stock.

You don't need expensive software to start. A well-built spreadsheet with these inputs beats most default reorder settings.
